## 1. Structural biases in preliminary macroeconomic data

Preliminary macroeconomic estimates are not random approximations to the truth. They are produced under institutional and informational constraints that introduce predictable, systematic patterns. The intuition that early data would simply be refined as more information arrives turns out to be empirically weak. Preliminary releases of GDP and monetary aggregates contain systematic departures from rationality, tending toward downward bias, excessive reliance on simple extrapolations of recent trends, and over-sensitivity to real-time seasonal adjustment procedures (Mankiw and Shapiro, 1986; Mankiw, Runkle and Shapiro, 1984; Mork, 1987; Kavajecz and Collins, 1995). Revision errors, then, are not a minor calibration problem but a deeper structural friction between what agencies can measure promptly and what they eventually know to be true.

This friction was recognized early. In 1978, Howrey showed formally that ignoring the distinction between preliminary and revised data when estimating econometric models leads to sub-optimal forecasts and unnecessarily large forecast errors. The finding that treating preliminary data as if it were final introduces a systematic handicap has since been confirmed across different national contexts. In a cross-country comparison of G-7 nations, Faust, Rogers and Wright (2005) showed that subsequent GDP revisions can be predicted using variables that were already available at the time of the first release, implying that statistical agencies do not incorporate all existing information into their initial estimates and that these estimates are therefore systematically improvable. Similarly, Fixler and Grimm (2002) documented persistent reliability challenges in US national accounts, especially around cyclical turning points, precisely the moments where policy relies most heavily on fresh data.

The production process itself is part of the explanation. Reconciling national accounts across different measurement approaches (income, expenditure and output) is a technically complex and lagged procedure. Achieving consistency between preliminary sectoral time series and benchmark revisions requires sophisticated statistical adjustments that mechanically generate corrections to earlier estimates (Chen, Di Fonzo, Howells and Marini, 2018). A non-trivial share of GDP revisions is therefore not informational; it is a by-product of the accounting architecture of national statistics.

## 2. Heteroscedastic and regime-dependent revision errors

Revision errors do not behave uniformly over time. They are persistent and heteroscedastic, with a variance that fluctuates according to the macroeconomic regime. This is arguably the most policy-relevant finding in the revision literature, because the very moments when decision-makers most need reliable real-time data are precisely the moments when that data is least reliable.

Aruoba (2008) demonstrated this rigorously, showing that US data revisions violate the assumptions of standard state-space models and exhibit structural heteroscedasticity. For Spain, Pavía, Cánovas and Caballero (2018) documented systematic seasonal patterns and clear regime dependence in the quality of GDP estimates, finding that the third quarter (Q3) carried the highest revision errors while the fourth quarter (Q4) showed virtually zero revisions. However, subsequent evidence and the inclusion of the COVID-19 shock reveal a more complex pattern: while Q3 remains the most volatile, Q4 revision errors are also structurally high. This discrepancy is partly driven by the massive volatility of the pandemic, but also reflects the retroactive impact of major historical benchmark revisions (Base 2015 and Base 2021) implemented by the Spanish statistical office (INE) after 2018, which revised historical Q4 estimates that were unadjusted in Pavía's original 1995–2016 vintage sample. The trade-off between timeliness and accuracy worsens dramatically during episodes of economic stress. As Asimakopoulos, Lalik, Paredes and Salvado (2023) have argued recently, the incentives facing statistical agencies create a structural bias toward publishing timely but less accurate estimates, and this bias becomes especially costly in crisis periods when the informational environment deteriorates most sharply.

This heteroscedasticity is not a statistical nuisance. It is the empirical motivation for our paper. If revision errors are systematically larger during periods of elevated uncertainty, there ought to be a real-time signal, computable ex ante, that tracks that variation. Finding and validating such a signal is the central contribution of this work.

## 3. The blind spot of existing uncertainty measures

Parallel to the revision literature, macroeconomics has invested heavily in building aggregate measures of uncertainty. The progression has been logical, moving from financial market proxies such as the VIX (Bloom, 2009), to macroeconomic indices that summarize the common unpredictable component across hundreds of series (Jurado, Ludvigson and Ng, 2015), and then to policy-based composite indices that combine newspaper coverage, tax code expiration dates and forecaster disagreement (Baker, Bloom and Davis, 2016). For Spain, Ghirelli, Gil, Pérez and Urtasun (2019) constructed a domestically calibrated version of the EPU index, showing that uncertainty measured this way correlates with investment slowdowns and GDP contractions.

These measures have proved valuable in macroeconomic research, but they share a common blind spot. They are designed to capture generalized economic risk, not the specific informational disorder that makes official statistics unreliable. The VIX signals market stress. The EPU captures policy ambiguity. Neither is designed to measure how much we should trust the most recent GDP flash estimate. Berge (2021) showed that real-time estimates of the output gap are subject to time-varying uncertainty that follows the business cycle, a finding closer to our concern, but even there the focus is on parameter uncertainty in a single model, not on the dispersion across a forecasting ensemble.

The most promising strand of uncertainty research, for our purposes, is the one focused on forecaster disagreement. The idea that the cross-sectional dispersion of professional forecasts contains information about aggregate uncertainty, beyond what a single model would indicate, has a long intellectual history (Mankiw, Reis and Wolfers, 2004). The formal justification was provided by Lahiri and Sheng (2010), who showed, through a decomposition of forecast errors into common and idiosyncratic components, that total forecast uncertainty is exactly equal to inter-forecaster disagreement plus the variance of future aggregate shocks. Disagreement is therefore a theoretically grounded component of epistemic ambiguity, not an empirical curiosity. Nevertheless, disagreement-based measures rely on surveys (such as the Survey of Professional Forecasters or Consensus Economics), which are infrequent, subject to behavioral biases, and structurally limited to the set of forecasters who choose to participate (Clark and Mertens, 2017; Carriero, Clark and Marcellino, 2018). An objective, model-based alternative is preferable for real-time monitoring purposes.

## 4. Connecting ex-ante uncertainty to ex-post statistical quality

Taken together, the two strands reviewed above point to a gap that neither has filled. The revision literature documents *that* official data degrades during crises. The uncertainty literature documents *that* economic uncertainty rises during crises. But the formal, operational connection between the two, a real-time ex-ante index that predicts ex-post data quality, has not been established.

The conceptual roots of this gap run deep. Knight (1921) distinguished between risk, where the probability distribution of outcomes is known and can be priced, and genuine uncertainty, where the distribution itself is unknown. In stable periods, forecasting systems operate essentially in the risk domain, with models agreeing on the likely trajectory of the economy and revisions to preliminary estimates remaining modest. In episodes of acute macroeconomic stress, the environment shifts toward what Ellsberg (1961) called ambiguity, a state in which models diverge, agents cannot agree on the relevant probability model, and the informational basis of any estimate collapses. This transition is precisely what generates the revision spikes documented in Section 3.

Formalized as an information-theoretic problem, the disorder of a forecasting system can be measured through entropy. Shannon entropy quantifies the spread of probability mass across possible outcomes, so that higher entropy corresponds to more disorder, less predictability, and, we argue, less trustworthy preliminary data. This approach has historical precursors in macroeconomics. Patterson and Heravi (1991) applied informational entropy to characterize how uncertainty diminishes as national accounts are revised over successive vintages. More recently, Shoja and Soofi (2017) showed that entropy-based measures are empirically superior to standard variance-based measures for capturing the meaningful disagreement among economic forecasters, since they are sensitive to the full shape of the distribution, not just its second moment. Methodologically, our construction of a composite entropy index from a multi-model ensemble relates to the frontier of forecast combination under uncertainty, where entropy has also proven to be an effective weighting criterion (Bretó, Espinosa, Hernández and Pavía, 2019).

Our framework operationalizes this intuition by constructing the CEUI, a composite real-time uncertainty index built from three dimensions of ensemble disorder (within-model variability, between-model dispersion, and temporal forecast instability). Unlike the unidimensional uncertainty proxies reviewed above, the CEUI is computable at the moment the flash estimate is published, contains no look-ahead bias, and is designed specifically to predict the subsequent magnitude of data revisions. The empirical validation of this link, and its superiority over the VIX and the EPU as predictors of Spanish GDP data quality, is the subject of Sections 4 through 6.
