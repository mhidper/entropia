\begin{abstract}
Statistical agencies face a fundamental trade-off between timeliness and accuracy, particularly during economic crises when real-time data becomes structurally disordered. This paper proposes a comprehensive information-theoretic framework to quantify this disorder through a multidimensional entropy index that decomposes predictive ambiguity into three sources: within-model variability, between-model dispersion, and temporal instability. Using a five-model ensemble (VAR, Random Forest, ARIMA, LSTM, and Dynamic Factor Models) applied to Spanish GDP growth between 2019Q1 and 2025Q1, we demonstrate that the entropy of the forecasting system provides a robust predictor of official revision errors in National Accounts. Our results extend the analysis of \cite{pavia2018} to the 2005--2025 period, identifying critical amplification factors of 1.8x, 3.6x, and 2.9x during major crises. We establish a strong correlation $(\rho = 0.732, p < 0.001)$ between entropic disorder and the magnitude of GDP revisions, with average absolute revisions becoming 6.2x larger under extreme uncertainty regimes. This evidence suggests that informational entropy not only captures economic ambiguity but serves as a leading indicator of statistical quality variations, extending the univariate revision metrics established in the Spanish real-time literature \citep{pavia2018}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modern macroeconomics and policy design rely on the prompt availability of National Accounts. However, statistical agencies such as Spain's INE (Instituto Nacional de Estadística) face an inherent trade-off between timeliness and precision \citep{mankiw1986}. The production of ``flash'' GDP estimates requires statistical triangulation under conditions of incomplete information, leading to revisions as newer and more comprehensive data becomes available. While revisions are a natural part of the statistical production cycle, their magnitude and direction are not uniform over time. As \citet{orphanides2001} noted, utilizing real-time data that is prone to large subsequent revisions can lead to significant policy missteps, particularly in monetary and fiscal calibration.

Recently, global disruptions—the 2008 financial crisis, the European sovereign debt crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic—have highlighted that this timeliness-accuracy trade-off is drastically amplified during periods of stress. Figure \ref{fig:volatility_ts} illustrates the temporal evolution of this revision volatility in Spanish GDP.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig1_revision_volatility.pdf}
    \caption{Temporal Evolution of Real-Time GDP Revision Volatility ($\sigma^{rev}_t$) in Spain (2005--2025). The shaded areas represent major crisis episodes: the Global Financial Crisis (2008--2009), the European Sovereign Debt Crisis (2010--2012), and the COVID-19 pandemic (2020--2022).}
    \label{fig:volatility_ts}
\end{figure}


In stable regimes, revisions to Spanish GDP growth are relatively small and centered around zero. However, our analysis reveals that the magnitude of these revisions (measured by Mean Absolute Error, MAE) spikes during crises, with amplification factors of 1.8x in 2008--09, 3.6x in 2010--12, and 2.9x in 2020--22 compared to normal periods. 
 During these episodes, statistical agencies are not merely dealing with missing data, but with a structural breakdown of the informational environment.

Despite the well-documented existence of these revision spikes \citep{pavia2018, croushore2011}, the precise mechanism through which economic uncertainty translates into statistical error remains largely unmeasured. The existing literature is divided into two distinct traditions: the \textit{revision literature}, which asks how much and when data is updated \citep{aruoba2008, asimakopoulos2023}, and the \textit{uncertainty measurement literature}, which seeks to quantify economic ambiguity using various proxies like the VIX \citep{bloomuncer}, text-based indices \citep{baker2016measuring}, or unpredictable volatility \citep{jurado2015}. This paper bridges these two fields by formalizing the link between the entropic state of a forecasting system and the quality of official statistics.

We hypothesize that the magnitude of revision errors reflects the informational disorder prevalent at the time of the initial estimate. To test this, we model the forecasting environment as a probabilistic system and measure its disorder through the differential entropy of an ensemble predictive distribution. The fundamental result of our theoretical framework is that this total entropy decomposes exactly into three components: (i) average internal model disorder (aleatory uncertainty), (ii) disagreement among competing modelling paradigms (epistemic uncertainty), and (iii) the rate of information destruction between consecutive periods (temporal instability).

Our contribution is threefold. First, we develop a theoretically grounded framework that maps information-theoretic concepts to observable forecasting statistics, providing a nuanced decomposition of predictive uncertainty. Second, we extend the analysis of National Accounts quality in Spain \citep{pavia2018} to cover the 2005--2025 period, documenting the evolution of revision patterns through three major crises. Third, we provide the first empirical evidence that informational entropy in the forecasting environment acts as a statistically significant predictor of GDP revision volatility ($\rho = 0.63, p < 0.001$). We show that under regimes of extreme entropic disorder, average absolute revisions in official statistics are 6.2x larger than in normal regimes.

The results suggest that the dispersion across models and the instability of revisions observed in institutional forecasting are not merely noise; they are informative signals about the underlying reliability of real-time macroeconomic data. For policymakers, our index provides an ex-ante measure of the level of caution that should be applied to current estimates before committing to structural policy shifts.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on data revisions and uncertainty measurement. Section 3 formalizes the entropic framework and its decomposition. Section 4 describes the vintage dataset and the forecasting ensemble. Section 5 presents the empirical results and the connection between entropy and statistical error. Section 6 provides robustness checks. Section 7 discusses policy implications, and Section 8 concludes.

\section{Literature Review and Methodological Context}

\subsection{The Quality of Official Statistics and Data Revisions}

The literature on data revisions has traditionally focused on characterizing the statistical properties of noise and news in economic series. \citet{mankiw1986} and \citet{orphanides2001} identified that revisions are not merely technical adjustments but central elements of policy risk. In the Spanish context, \citet{pavia2018} provided a comprehensive evaluation of GDP quality, showing systematic patterns of revision that vary by quarter and economic regime. More recently, \citet{asimakopoulos2023} has highlighted that the timeliness of these revisions is increasingly critical in globalized economies with high-frequency shocks. 

However, as \citet{aruoba2008} demonstrates, revision errors are often not ``well-behaved''—they exhibit persistence and heteroscedasticity that are not captured by standard state-space models. This suggests that the quality of official statistics is endogenous to the level of informational disorder in the macroeconomic environment.

\subsection{Economic Uncertainty and the Informational Environment}

Parallel to the revision literature, the quantification of economic uncertainty has seen rapid advancement. \citet{bloomuncer} pioneered the use of market volatility (VIX) as a proxy for uncertainty shocks. \citet{jurado2015} shifted the focus towards the common unpredictable component of large datasets, formalizing uncertainty as the conditional volatility of many variables. 

While these measures are highly effective at capturing generalized risk, they often neglect the specific uncertainty originating from the forecasting system itself. \cite{clark2017} and \cite{carriero2018} have used survey-based forecasts to measure disagreement, but their reliance on subjective expectations limits their ability to capture structural informational disorder.

\subsection{The Missing Link: Uncertainty and Revision Error}

The central gap in the existing literature—the ``missing link''—is the formal connection between ex-ante uncertainty and ex-post statistical quality. While it is intuitive that revisions should be larger during crises, the literature lacks a unified metric that is systematically associated with this breakdown.

Our framework fills this gap by introducing informational entropy as a sufficient statistic for the state of the forecasting environment. By decomposing total entropy into model dispersion, within-model variability, and temporal instability, we provide a bridge between the structural ambiguity described by \citet{ellsberg1961} and the practical measurement of statistical quality. Unlike unidimensional measures, our CEUI exhibits a strong contemporaneous and lagged association with the magnitude of data revisions.


This multidimensional approach provides several advantages: (1) comprehensive uncertainty capture across different sources, (2) robustness to model misspecification through diverse methodologies, (3) actionable insights through dimension-specific analysis, and (4) enhanced crisis detection through complementary uncertainty signals. Figure \ref{fig:ceui_evolution} shows the resulting composite index.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

\subsection{Uncertainty as Disorder in a Forecasting System}

Economic forecasting during periods of crisis is not merely difficult—it is
structurally disordered. As structural breaks propagate through the
economy, different models reach increasingly divergent conclusions, each
model's own predictions become less precise, and the forecasts themselves
become increasingly unstable over time. The aggregate effect is a
forecasting system that generates more \emph{surprise}—in the precise
information-theoretic sense—than during periods of stability.

This paper formalizes that intuition. We model the forecasting environment
as a probabilistic system and measure its disorder through the entropy of
the ensemble predictive distribution. The central result of this section is
that this entropy decomposes \emph{exactly} into three components that
correspond one-to-one with our empirical measures: within-model
variability, between-model dispersion, and temporal instability.

\subsection{The Forecasting System as a Probabilistic Ensemble}

Let $y_{t+h}$ denote the target variable (Spanish GDP growth) at horizon
$h$. At time $t$, a set of $K$ forecasting models $\{M_1, \ldots, M_K\}$
is estimated on the information set $\mathcal{I}_t$. Each model $M_k$
generates a predictive distribution:

\begin{equation}
    Q_k \equiv Q_k(y_{t+h} \mid \mathcal{I}_t), \quad k = 1, \ldots, K
    \label{eq:predictive_dist}
\end{equation}

A forecaster who does not know which model is correct—or who wishes to
aggregate over competing specifications—faces the \emph{ensemble
predictive distribution}, defined as the equally-weighted mixture:

\begin{equation}
    Q_{\text{ens},t} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} Q_k
    \label{eq:ensemble}
\end{equation}

The information content of this ensemble—the expected surprise of
observing a realization drawn from it—is measured by its differential
entropy:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}(Q_{\text{ens},t}) = -\int Q_{\text{ens},t}(y)
    \ln Q_{\text{ens},t}(y)\, dy
    \label{eq:total_entropy}
\end{equation}

We define $\mathcal{H}(Q_{\text{ens},t})$ as the \textbf{total predictive
uncertainty} of the forecasting system at time $t$. The higher this
quantity, the more disordered the system's collective assessment of the
future.

\subsection{The Entropy Decomposition}

The fundamental result that structures our framework is the following
decomposition of the mixture entropy \citep{haussler1997,heskes1998}:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}(Q_{\text{ens},t}) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{K}
    \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathcal{H}(Q_k)}_{\mathcal{U}^{\text{within}}_t}
    \;+\;
    \underbrace{D_{\text{JS}}(Q_1, \ldots, Q_K)}_{\mathcal{U}^{\text{between}}_t}
    \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}

where $D_{\text{JS}}$ denotes the Jensen--Shannon divergence of the
individual distributions around their mixture:

\begin{equation}
    D_{\text{JS}}(Q_1,\ldots,Q_K) = \mathcal{H}(Q_{\text{ens},t})
    - \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathcal{H}(Q_k) \;\geq\; 0
    \label{eq:jsd}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:decomposition} is an exact, not approximate,
identity. Its interpretation is direct:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{U}^{\text{within}}_t$ measures the \textbf{average
    internal disorder} of the individual models. It is high when each model,
    taken in isolation, is uncertain about its own prediction. This
    corresponds to \emph{aleatory} or irreducible uncertainty: even the
    best individual model cannot resolve it.

    \item $\mathcal{U}^{\text{between}}_t$ measures the \textbf{disorder
    arising from disagreement} among the models. It is high when the models
    reach substantially different conclusions despite sharing the same
    information set. This corresponds to \emph{epistemic} uncertainty: it
    would vanish if we knew which model was correct.
\end{itemize}

The non-negativity of $D_{\text{JS}}$ guarantees that mixing always
increases entropy: a forecaster who aggregates multiple models is always
at least as uncertain as the average individual model, with the difference
measuring precisely the cost of model disagreement.

\subsection{Temporal Instability as Entropic Dynamics}

Equation~\eqref{eq:decomposition} characterizes the static disorder of the
system at a given point in time. To capture the \emph{dynamic} dimension
of uncertainty—how much the system's assessment changes as new information
arrives—we introduce a third component based on the informational distance
between consecutive ensemble distributions.

Define the \textbf{temporal entropic shift} as the Kullback--Leibler
divergence of the current ensemble from the previous period's ensemble:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{U}^{\text{temporal}}_t =
    D_{\text{KL}}(Q_{\text{ens},t} \;\|\; Q_{\text{ens},t-1})
    = \int Q_{\text{ens},t}(y)
    \ln \frac{Q_{\text{ens},t}(y)}{Q_{\text{ens},t-1}(y)}\, dy
    \label{eq:temporal}
\end{equation}

$\mathcal{U}^{\text{temporal}}_t$ measures the amount of information that
the updated ensemble at $t$ contains relative to the ensemble at $t-1$.
Equivalently, it quantifies the \emph{surprise} an agent would experience
if they held last period's beliefs and observed the current revision. A
forecasting system in a stable regime generates low temporal instability:
the models update smoothly and little information is lost or gained between
periods. A system experiencing a structural break generates high temporal
instability: the accumulated information from prior periods is suddenly of
little use, and the system must re-learn the economy's dynamics almost from
scratch.

This component is conceptually distinct from the static decomposition in
equation~\eqref{eq:decomposition}: it measures not \emph{how disordered}
the system is at a given moment, but \emph{how rapidly} that disorder is
changing. Together, the three components provide a complete characterization
of uncertainty as perceived by an ensemble of competing forecasting models.

\subsection{Gaussian Approximation and Operational Measures}
\label{sec:gaussian_approx}

Computing the quantities in equations~\eqref{eq:decomposition}
and~\eqref{eq:temporal} exactly requires full predictive distributions.
In practice, our five models yield predictive distributions that are
either explicitly Gaussian (VAR, DFM via Kalman filtering) or well
approximated by Gaussians for the purpose of uncertainty quantification
(LSTM via Monte Carlo dropout, ARIMA via asymptotic theory, Random
Forest via bootstrap intervals). We therefore work under the following
assumption throughout the empirical analysis.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Assumption G (Gaussian predictive distributions).} Each model
$M_k$ generates a predictive distribution $Q_k = \mathcal{N}(\hat{y}_k,
\sigma_k^2)$, where $\hat{y}_k$ is the point forecast and $\sigma_k$ is
the associated predictive standard deviation.
\end{quote}

Under Assumption~G, the three components of predictive uncertainty admit closed-form
or analytically tractable expressions that map directly to observable
forecasting statistics. We state these as a formal proposition.

\begin{proposition}[Operational Equivalences under Gaussianity]
\label{prop:operational}
Under Assumption~G, the three components of predictive uncertainty satisfy:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Within-model uncertainty:}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{U}^{\text{within}}_t =
        \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathcal{H}(\mathcal{N}(\hat{y}_k,
        \sigma_k^2)) = \frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi e) +
        \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \ln \sigma_k
        \label{eq:within_gaussian}
    \end{equation}
    This is a monotone increasing function of the average log-predictive
    standard deviation. Since the 95\% confidence interval width equals
    $3.92\,\sigma_k$, this component is directly observable from the
    confidence intervals produced by each model.

    \item \textbf{Between-model dispersion:} Under the additional
    assumption of homogeneous variance $\sigma_k^2 = \bar{\sigma}^2$
    for all $k$, the Jensen--Shannon divergence satisfies:
    \begin{equation}
        D_{\text{JS}}(Q_1,\ldots,Q_K) \approx
        \frac{1}{2\bar{\sigma}^2} \cdot
        \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\hat{y}_k - \bar{y})^2
        = \frac{D_{t+h|t}^2}{2\bar{\sigma}^2}
        \label{eq:between_gaussian}
    \end{equation}
    where $D_{t+h|t}$ is the empirical standard deviation of point
    forecasts across models. The
    between-model entropy component is thus proportional to the squared
    forecast dispersion, scaled by the common predictive variance.

    \item \textbf{Temporal instability:} Considering consecutive Gaussian
    ensembles $Q_{\text{ens},t} = \mathcal{N}(\bar{y}_t, \bar{\sigma}_t^2)$
    and $Q_{\text{ens},t-1} = \mathcal{N}(\bar{y}_{t-1},
    \bar{\sigma}_{t-1}^2)$, the KL divergence takes the closed form:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{U}^{\text{temporal}}_t =
        \frac{(\bar{y}_t - \bar{y}_{t-1})^2}{2\bar{\sigma}_{t-1}^2}
        + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\bar{\sigma}_t^2}{\bar{\sigma}_{t-1}^2}
        - 1 - \ln\frac{\bar{\sigma}_t^2}{\bar{\sigma}_{t-1}^2}\right)
        \label{eq:temporal_gaussian}
    \end{equation}
    The first term captures the informational cost of the revision in the
    ensemble mean—the larger the revision relative to the prior period's
    uncertainty, the greater the entropic shift. The second term captures
    changes in the ensemble's overall dispersion between periods.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:operational} establishes that the empirical measures
computed in Section~4—confidence interval widths, forecast dispersion, and
forecast revisions—are not ad hoc statistics but direct approximations to
well-defined information-theoretic quantities. The composite uncertainty
index constructed in Section~4.4 can therefore be interpreted as an
estimate of the total predictive entropy of the forecasting system,
normalized to a common scale.

\begin{remark}
The analytical approximation for the Jensen-Shannon Divergence in Proposition 1 assumes local homogeneity of the model-specific predictive variances. While the exact JSD can be computed through numerical integration of the mixture density, a formal verification remains beyond the scope of the current analysis, as it would require model-specific predictive variances that are not uniformly available across all five models. However, the dominance of mean separation during crisis episodes suggests that the approximation error is small in the empirically relevant region of the parameter space.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Economic Interpretation}

The three components map naturally onto the sources of forecasting failure
observed during macroeconomic crises.

\textbf{Within-model variability} ($\mathcal{U}^{\text{within}}$) reflects
the inherent stochasticity of the economic environment that no model can
eliminate. It increases when structural volatility rises—as in the initial
COVID-19 shock—or when parameter uncertainty accumulates following a
structural break. A persistently high $\mathcal{U}^{\text{within}}$ after
a crisis episode is a signal that the economy has not returned to its
pre-crisis stochastic regime.

\textbf{Between-model dispersion} ($\mathcal{U}^{\text{between}}$) reflects
the epistemic uncertainty that arises when competing analytical frameworks
reach incompatible conclusions. In our setting, this takes a particularly
informative form: the VAR, grounded in linear time-series dynamics, and
the Random Forest, designed to capture structural non-linearities, will
produce systematically different responses to an unprecedented shock.
High dispersion is therefore a signal of model-structural uncertainty—the
forecasting community does not agree on \emph{which} economic model applies
in the current regime.

\textbf{Temporal instability} ($\mathcal{U}^{\text{temporal}}$) measures
the rate at which economic knowledge is being destroyed and reconstructed.
A large KL divergence between consecutive ensembles means that agents who
held yesterday's beliefs are poorly positioned today—the economy has
generated information that invalidates prior assessments. This is precisely
what occurs at the onset of a recession or financial crisis: the
information accumulated during the preceding stable period is suddenly of
limited relevance, and the forecasting system must adapt rapidly to a new
informational environment.

Together, these three dimensions allow us to distinguish between crises
that are \emph{noisy but predictable} (high $\mathcal{U}^{\text{within}}$,
low $\mathcal{U}^{\text{between}}$), crises that are \emph{structurally
ambiguous} (low $\mathcal{U}^{\text{within}}$, high
$\mathcal{U}^{\text{between}}$), and crises that are
\emph{dynamically evolving} (high $\mathcal{U}^{\text{temporal}}$). The
COVID-19 episode, as our empirical analysis demonstrates, was all three
simultaneously—which is precisely why it generated the most extreme values
of the composite index observed in the sample.

\section{Data and Implementation}

\subsection{CNTR Vintage Dataset}
\label{sec:cntr_data}

Our primary dataset for the revision analysis is the real-time database of Spain's
Contabilidad Nacional Trimestral (CNTR), obtained from the Instituto Nacional de
Estad\'istica (INE). The dataset comprises a vintage triangle of 144 quarterly
observations spanning 1995Q1--2025Q1,\footnote{The inclusion of 2024Q4 and 2025Q1 allows for an updated assessment of the current regime. However, readers should be aware of a potential maturity bias: these periods have undergone significantly fewer revisions compared to the historical average. Our robustness checks in Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness_maturity} demonstrate that the relationship between CEUI and revision volatility remains statistically significant when controlling for the number of available vintages ($p<0.001$) or when excluding quarters with fewer than five revisions ($p<0.001$).} with 107 publication vintages identified by
their exact release timestamp (from November 2005 to June 2025). Each vintage
corresponds to an official CNTR publication and contains the full historical GDP
level series at that point in time.

The target variable is the quarter-on-quarter GDP growth rate (seasonally adjusted),
computed as the percentage change in the GDP level index relative to the preceding
quarter. All revision metrics are computed on this growth rate series.

We define two operationally precise concepts. The \textbf{flash estimate}
($y_{t,\text{flash}}$) is the first vintage published after the reference quarter $t$
has ended. The \textbf{final estimate} ($y_{t,\text{final}}$) is the most recent
vintage available for each quarter. The \textbf{revision} for quarter $t$ is defined
as $r_t = y_{t,\text{final}} - y_{t,\text{flash}}$.

For the cross-sectional volatility analysis we additionally define the
\textbf{revision standard deviation} $\sigma^{\text{rev}}_t$ as the standard deviation
of all available vintage estimates for quarter $t$. This metric uses the full
information content of the vintage triangle and is the key dependent variable in our
entropy--revision regressions (Section~\ref{sec:entropy_revisions}).


\subsection{Data Sources and Sample}

Our empirical analysis focuses on Spanish GDP quarterly data from 2000Q1 to 2025Q1, providing a comprehensive dataset spanning multiple economic cycles including the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. The choice of Spain provides an ideal testing ground for our framework, as the Spanish economy experienced significant volatility during both crisis periods, offering rich variation in uncertainty levels.

The target variable is the Spanish GDP growth rate (quarter-on-quarter, seasonally adjusted), obtained from the Instituto Nacional de Estadaística (INE). To construct our multidimensional uncertainty framework, we employ a comprehensive set of predictor variables covering three main economic domains. Labor market dynamics are captured through indicators such as total employment, hours worked, and Social Security affiliations. Business activity is monitored via industrial production indices for manufacturing and intermediate goods, as well as PMI indicators for both manufacturing and services. Additionally, we include service sector indicators such as business turnover indices for transportation, professional services, and hospitality.

All series undergo standardized preprocessing including logarithmic transformation (where appropriate), seasonal adjustment using X-13 ARIMA-SEATS methodology, and conversion to growth rates to ensure stationarity. The final dataset comprises 11 predictor variables selected based on their correlation with GDP growth and data availability constraints.

\subsection{Model Specifications}

Our framework integrates five distinct forecasting models, each contributing unique perspectives on economic uncertainty:

\subsubsection{Vector Autoregression (VAR)}
We implement a VAR model with 4 lags and 11 variables.\footnote{As shown in the robustness check in Appendix~\ref{sec:robustness_var}, we verify that this relatively large specification (495 parameters for $\sim$80 observations) out-performs more parsimonious models as a sensor for informational entropy.} While information criteria (BIC) favor more parsimonious specifications in small samples, we fix the lag at $k=4$ to ensure that the annual seasonal memory characteristic of quarterly national accounts is fully captured. This specification follows standard practices in macroeconomic forecasting for Spain (INE) to avoid missing persistence in the data-generating process. Dimensionality concerns are mitigated by the aggregate ensemble architecture, where the VAR acts as our primary econometric baseline for linear within-model uncertainty. The model specification follows:

$$\mathbf{Y}_t = \mathbf{c} + \sum_{i=1}^{4} \mathbf{A}_i \mathbf{Y}_{t-i} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t$$

where $\mathbf{Y}_t$ is the vector of endogenous variables and $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t \sim N(0, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$.

\subsubsection{Random Forest}
The Random Forest implementation uses 50 trees with maximum depth of 3, minimum samples per split of 2, and rolling window estimation. This machine learning approach captures non-linear relationships and provides robust out-of-sample performance, particularly during crisis periods. Feature importance is automatically determined through the Gini impurity criterion.

\subsubsection{ARIMA}
We employ automatic ARIMA specification with model selection based on information criteria, testing combinations of $(p,d,q)$ parameters up to order 3. This univariate approach focuses solely on GDP dynamics, providing a pure time-series benchmark for model dispersion analysis.

\subsubsection{Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)}
The LSTM architecture employs 6-quarter input sequences with two LSTM layers (24 and 24 units), dropout regularization (20\%), and dense output layer. The model is trained for 25 epochs with early stopping based on validation loss, capturing complex temporal dependencies and non-linear patterns in multivariate economic data.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Factor Model (DFM)}
We implement a DFM with 2 common factors estimated using Kalman filtering and maximum likelihood estimation. The model reduces dimensionality while preserving information content:

$$\mathbf{Y}_t = \boldsymbol{\Lambda} \mathbf{F}_t + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t$$
$$\mathbf{F}_t = \mathbf{A}(L) \mathbf{F}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{\eta}_t$$

where $\mathbf{F}_t$ represents the common factors and $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ the factor loadings matrix.

\subsection{Rolling Evaluation Protocol}

The empirical evaluation follows a real-time forecasting protocol designed to reflect the information constraints faced by policymakers. Specifically, each model is estimated using a training window that incorporates all available data up to period $t-1$. We focus on one-quarter-ahead predictions for period $t$ over an evaluation period spanning 2019Q1 to 2025Q1, comprising 25 individual quarters. Performance is assessed through standard metrics, including Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). Uncertainty is quantified through 95\% confidence intervals for the VAR, prediction intervals for the LSTM model, and the ensemble variance across all constituent models.

This approach ensures that our uncertainty measures reflect real-time decision-making constraints and avoid look-ahead bias in the evaluation process.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:implementation}

The construction of the CEUI follows a sequence of rigorous informational steps designed to maintain structural consistency across regimes. After generating the daily/quarterly forecasts from the five-model ensemble, we compute the three primary dimensions of uncertainty ($\mathcal{U}^{\text{within}}$, $\mathcal{U}^{\text{between}}$, and $\mathcal{U}^{\text{temporal}}$). 

A critical design choice is the use of \textbf{percentile normalization} (0--100) instead of raw Shannon entropy (bits). While the bit-scale preserves the theoretical sum $\mathcal{H}_{total} = \sum \mathcal{U}_i$, it is highly sensitive to the absolute scale of variances, which varies dramatically between pre- and post-COVID regimes. The percentile mapping acts as an \emph{informational invariant filter}, ensuring that a value of 80 represents the top quintile of informational disorder in both stable and volatile periods. This choice facilitates operational clarity without sacrificing the ordinal signal of system-wide entropy.

Regarding the aggregation of the composite index, we employ an \textbf{unweighted sum} of the three dimensions (weights of 1/3 each). This choice follows an agnostic Bayesian perspective: in the absence of a consistently superior model or a stable regime-dependent ranking of uncertainty types, a uniform prior is the most robust strategy for capturing system-wide ambiguity. Although our audit shows that model-specific forecast variances are not strictly homogeneous (with the VAR exhibiting higher nominal volatility), the Random Forest and LSTM capture higher non-linear risk during tail events. Thus, the unweighted average appropriately reflects the mean informativeness of the ensemble under structural uncertainty.

The entire framework is implemented in Python using scikit-learn, statsmodels, and TensorFlow libraries, ensuring reproducibility and computational efficiency.

\subsection{Limitations}

Despite the contributions of our framework, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the out-of-sample evaluation period comprises only 25 quarters (2019Q1--2025Q1), a relatively small sample that may limit the statistical power of the regressions in Table \ref{tab:entropy_regression} and the precision of the regime classification thresholds. Future studies spanning longer horizons could enhance the robustness of these inferences. Second, we rely on the assumption of Gaussianity for the predictive distributions. While the VAR and DFM generate naturally Gaussian forecasts, the intervals for the Random Forest and LSTM models are based on bootstrap and Monte Carlo dropout approximations, respectively, and are thus only approximately normal. Relaxing this assumption through non-parametric methods, such as kernel density estimation, represents a natural extension of this work.

An important caveat concerns causal interpretation. The documented association between the CEUI and revision volatility should not be read as implying that entropy causes larger revisions. Both variables are plausibly driven by a common underlying factor: the magnitude of the economic shock itself. A large adverse shock simultaneously increases model disagreement---raising the CEUI---and makes initial GDP estimates less accurate---raising revision volatility. Our lagged specification (Table~\ref{tab:horserace}) provides suggestive evidence of temporal precedence, but cannot resolve the identification problem without an exogenous source of variation in informational entropy that is independent of the economic shock. We therefore characterise the relationship as a robust predictive association rather than a structural causal mechanism.

Third, the documented relationship between system entropy and INE revisions
 should be interpreted as predictive correlation rather than direct causality, as both could be driven by underlying economic shocks. Finally, as this analysis is confined to the Spanish economic context, different institutional compilation processes in other countries may yield diverse entropy-revision dynamics, warranting further cross-country comparative research.

The tight confidence intervals and low bootstrap standard deviations confirm that the thresholds are statistically stable throughout the sample. This stability is crucial for operational policy design, as it ensures that the transition between uncertainty regimes reflects a genuine change in the economic environment rather than sampling noise in the index construction.

\section{Policy Implications and Applications}

\subsection{Actionable Guidance for Policymakers: The 'Stop-and-Wait' Signal}

The primary policy implication of our framework is that ex-ante uncertainty metrics must be integrated as filtering mechanisms for real-time policy responses. When the CEUI enters the 'High' regime ($>62$), our results suggest that official data is structurally unstable. In such environments, policymakers should adopt a 'stop-and-wait' posture, delaying structural decision-making until revisions stabilize. Committing to irreversible fiscal or monetary shifts based on estimates subject to 0.6 pp revisions—as observed during COVID-19—can lead to pro-cyclical errors that exacerbate the initial shock.

\subsection{Operational Guidance by Dimension}

The multidimensional decomposition of the CEUI provides specific operational guidance for the type of policy intervention required based on the nature of the prevailing informational disorder. When between-model dispersion dominates the index, the signal points to fundamental structural ambiguity. In this environment, the structural parameters of the economy are essentially unknown, suggesting that policymakers should prioritize communicative clarity and favor robust, simple policies over fine-tuned optimizations that rely on specific model assumptions.

In contrast, when within-model variability is the primary contributor to the uncertainty signal, it indicates a rise in aleatory risk. This reflects a period of heightened structural shocks where traditional insurance mechanisms, such as automatic stabilizers and contingency buffers, should be activated to manage the increased volatility. Finally, a dominance of temporal instability signals a rapid regime shift where economic knowledge is being quickly invalidated. In these scenarios, policy frameworks must increase their frequency of evaluation, as the high rate of information destruction implies that even month-old data may be of limited relevance for tomorrow's decisions.

\subsection{Institutional Resilience and Model Auditing}

Finally, our findings regarding the 'trauma' of linear models (VAR) underscore the need for institutional auditing of forecasting systems. Public statistical and economic agencies should move towards ensemble-based architectures. The superior resilience of Random Forest models during the pandemic demonstrates that robustness to structural breaks is not merely a statistical luxury but an operational necessity for maintaining the reliability of official economic guidance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presents a unified framework that bridges two previously disconnected
literatures: the analysis of data revisions in National Accounts and the measurement
of economic uncertainty. Our central contribution is to document a statistically significant and robust association between the informational entropy of the forecasting system and the magnitude of revisions.

Our findings can be summarised in five points. First, the replication and extension
of \citet{pavia2018} to the 2005--2025 period confirms that the seasonal pattern
$\text{MAE}_{Q4} \approx \text{MAE}_{Q3} < \text{MAE}_{Q1}$ is a structural feature
of the CNTR compilation process, persistent across 30 years and three crisis episodes.
Second, the amplification factors of 1.8x (Global Financial Crisis), 3.6x (Sovereign
Debt Crisis), and 2.9x (COVID-19) establish a clear hierarchy of statistical quality
degradation across crises. The sovereign debt crisis produced the largest amplification,
reflecting its protracted and structurally ambiguous nature. Third, our entropic
decomposition formalises uncertainty as the sum of aleatory disorder
($\mathcal{U}^{\text{within}}$) and epistemic disorder
($\mathcal{U}^{\text{between}} = D_{\text{JS}}$), grounded in the exact mixture
entropy decomposition of \citet{haussler1997}. Fourth, the Composite Economic
Uncertainty Index (CEUI) exhibits a strong and statistically significant correlation
with revision volatility ($\rho = 0.732$, $p < 0.001$), with average absolute revisions
6.2 times larger under extreme uncertainty regimes than under normal conditions.
Fifth, the between-model dispersion component ($\mathcal{U}^{\text{between}}$) is
the dominant predictor of revision errors, suggesting that the fundamental source of
statistical quality degradation is epistemic---the inability of the forecasting
community to agree on which model governs the economy during a crisis.

For policymakers and statistical agencies, these results carry a direct implication:
the CEUI provides an ex-ante signal of the reliability of current GDP flash estimates.
When the index enters the elevated or extreme regime, users of National Accounts data
should apply substantially greater caution before acting on preliminary figures. The
evidence on institutional learning---the lower amplification factor of COVID-19
relative to the sovereign debt crisis---suggests that the INE improved its estimation
processes between episodes, a finding that the framework can help track systematically over time.

A companion paper, currently in preparation, explores the structural implications of informational entropy for optimal fiscal policy design within a DSGE framework.
